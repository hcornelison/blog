It's a great question, and one historians and economists actually argue about. The honest answer depends on what "worse" means: physical danger and material deprivation, or economic trajectory and whether life turned out better than expected. Those two measures point to different cohorts.

## So who had it worst?

**By raw hardship, it's those born roughly 1910–1925.** They grew up hungry during the Depression. Their medicine had no antibiotics, and polio had no vaccine. Then the young men were sent to the deadliest war in American history. No later cohort comes close in the odds of dying young or going without food.

**By economic trajectory and broken expectations, the case shifts to those born in the 1980s and 1990s.** Economist Raj Chetty's research found that about 90% of Americans born in 1940 ended up earning more than their parents. For those born in the mid-1980s, it was about 50%, a coin flip. Your cohort did everything it was told (school, college, work) and hit a financial crisis, stagnant wages, and housing prices that earlier generations never faced at the same age. You're far safer than a 1920s kid, but the promise that effort leads to steady progress broke for you in a way it didn't for them.

One more pattern stands out. The Depression and WWII cohorts had a terrible start and a very good second half: a long boom, pensions, cheap homes. Hard beginnings don't lock in a hard life.

## On the exhaustion

At your age, you've lived through 9/11, two decades of war, the 2008 collapse, a pandemic, and the fastest technological change in human history, from dial-up to AI. That's a lot, and feeling worn down by it is a reasonable reaction.

One difference is worth naming. Earlier generations faced bigger physical dangers, but they mostly learned about crises through a morning paper and an evening broadcast. Your generation takes in every crisis everywhere, constantly, on a device in your pocket. Part of what makes today feel so heavy is that load of constant exposure, and it's partly within your control in a way the Depression wasn't for someone born in 1915.

The perspective the older generations offer isn't "stop complaining, we had it worse." It's closer to this: people lived through enormous upheaval, still built good lives, and many of them saw their best years come after the hardest ones.
